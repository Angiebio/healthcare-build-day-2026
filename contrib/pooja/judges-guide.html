Judges guide

The two-minute version of what makes Lantern different, the honest edges of what we built in five hours, and a single command you can run to watch every claim on this page verify itself.

Why this is innovative

Most teams answering "make imaging discoverable across hospitals" build a smarter search box over the fields that already exist: modality, body part, a diagnosis string. Lantern does something no catalogue in that room can do today.

1 · A new queryable axis, compiled out of prose. 78.4% of the reports carry quantitative measurements written into sentences — atrial widths, ejection fractions, gestational age. A radiologist already measured them; they were simply invisible to search and unsafe to share. We compile them into typed, searchable facts inside the hospital boundary. You can now ask "fetal MR, lateral ventricular atrial width > 10 mm" — a query that returns real cohorts and that exists nowhere else.

2 · Utility up, exposure down — not a trade. The compiler ships less free text than a hospital does today (none) while surfacing more usable signal. Privacy and utility usually pull against each other; here the compilation happens before anything leaves, so both move the same direction.

3 · The disclosure boundary is the hospital, not a central index. k-anonymity is enforced per node: sixteen severe cases exist across the network — enough to clear the threshold together — but no single hospital's sub-ten cohort is ever exposed. That is what makes this a federation and not a warehouse with a login.

4 · Every fact shows its work. Each measurement carries its confidence and the exact clause it was read from, stamped report_extraction. A model's guess never gets to wear a clinical fact's clothes — the number is auditable against the sentence, without releasing the record.

Existing systems ask "may I have the scan?" Lantern asks "what does the scan already know, and how much of that can you have right now, without the scan moving at all?"

Verify every claim yourself

We would rather you check than take our word. From a clean clone, one command drives all four demo beats against the live API and asserts the numbers:

python -m app.run_all          # three hospital nodes + the broker
python tools/verify_demo.py    # in another terminal

It confirms, in order: federation answering from all three hospitals (EF < 40% = 30 / 53 / 73), the impossible query (fetal atrial width > 10 mm = 87 / 78 / 60), every match carrying provenance and a source snippet, severity > 15 mm suppressing at every node with no exact count on the wire, a petition routing to the owning hospital and writing an append-only audit entry, and a differencing attack being refused. The hard questions, answered

You might askOur answer
Extraction from free text will make mistakes. Yes. That is why every measurement carries a confidence and the exact source clause, and never stands alone — a researcher sees the sentence it came from. Before deployment we would validate against a radiologist-adjudicated set. It is extracted evidence, not clinically validated.
Below the k threshold, is the count just hidden in the interface? No — it is absent from the response. The policy engine is fail-closed: the rule fires the same way every time, and if anything goes wrong in that code path it suppresses, it never serves. The threshold (10) is a config constant surfaced in every response. You get a band, never a number.
k-anonymity is defeated by differencing. It is, so we built the defense: canonical query fingerprints detect an attacker subtracting two near-identical queries to isolate a rare cohort, and the response degrades. Tested against one-constraint subtraction; multi-axis and cross-session attacks are named limitations, not solved problems.
Isn't this just search over metadata? It is search over facts that exist as data nowhere today. We manufactured a queryable axis out of prose that was previously unusable — inside the boundary, so the prose stays home.
What about the actual pixels / full DICOM? Architecturally absent. We do not serve them and never hold them. A petition routes to the owning hospital with IRB and purpose captured, writes an append-only audit entry, and the hospital remains the enforcement point. Approval returns a simulated retrieval grant, not a live endpoint — and we say so.
Why should a hospital trust the pipeline? It doesn't trust it, it audits it. Every passport ships a de-identification manifest listing exactly what was removed, generalized, and pseudonymized. The evidence travels with the data.

What we deliberately did not do

Naming our own limits is the credibility play. This is PS3.15-aligned field-minimization, not a certified de-identification — our input is the challenge's JSON metadata, not DICOM objects, and we do not claim HIPAA compliance, because Safe Harbor and Expert Determination are the only two roads and neither is a five-hour exercise. No LLM sits in the request path — natural language is validated into a query, never interpreted. There are no image embeddings, no acquisition-parameter indexing, and no similarity search, because the supplied corpus has no pixels or acquisition fields. No real patient data was used at any point; every record is synthetic, deliberately, on day one. Three real services, real fan-out — running on one laptop.

How it maps to the rubric

Business & operational strength. The bottleneck we solve — discovery welded to access — is Boston Children's stated pain. The buyer is the hospital research office; the value is months of cohort-scoping collapsed to a query, with the privacy office's evidence built in.

Technical innovation & execution. A per-node privacy compiler, federated fan-out with per-node k-anon, rank fusion with explainability, and a working differencing defense — all deterministic, all verifiable with one command.

Alignment with the champion. Computation goes to the data; only de-identified derivatives return — the same thesis Boston Children's ChRIS is built on. The de-identification evidence, not the search box, is the hero.

Presentation. A four-beat demo that runs live, a two-minute video, and this page — every number on it re-derivable by you in one command.
