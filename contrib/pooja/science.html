Scientific choices

Every design decision below was made against measured properties of the data rather than assumptions about it. Where we chose wrong at first, that is recorded too.

We profiled before we designed

The first work on this project was not architecture. It was measurement. We characterised the supplied corpus of 2,700 studies across three simulated hospital nodes before writing a line of system code, and what we found redirected the entire build.

Two properties mattered. First, the corpus contains no pixel data and no acquisition parameters. There is no field strength, no repetition or echo time, no slice geometry, no reconstruction kernel. Every architecture we had sketched in advance assumed those fields, and every one of those designs was therefore unbuildable. Radiomic features, image embeddings, and similarity search all depend on data this corpus does not contain.

Second, and far more useful, 78.4 percent of the radiology reports carry quantitative clinical measurements written into the prose. Ventricular atrial widths in millimetres. Ejection fractions as percentages. Gestational age in weeks. Lesion dimensions in centimetres.

A radiologist had already measured a fetal lateral ventricular atrial width at 12.4 mm. The number was sitting in the third sentence of a paragraph, which made it simultaneously unreachable by search and unsafe to release.

That observation became the thesis. The scientifically valuable content and the privacy hazard were the same object, and separating them was the problem worth solving.

Why extraction happens before disclosure, not after

The intuitive privacy move is to delete the risky field. A free-text radiology report is the densest concentration of identifying information in an imaging record, so removing it protects the patient. It also destroys the research value, because the measurements live inside it.

Compiling resolves that dilemma. The extraction runs inside the institution, where the sensitive text is already lawfully held, and only the derived facts are eligible to leave. The result is a system where utility increases while exposure decreases, which is unusual, and it happens for a structural reason rather than a clever one. We are not choosing a better point on a tradeoff curve. We are computing before discarding instead of after.

Deterministic where correctness matters

Everything that decides what gets released is plain, tested Python. Measurement parsing, unit normalisation, age generalisation, the k-anonymity threshold, the disclosure guard, and the release policy are all deterministic functions with unit tests. None of them involve a model.

This is a deliberate stance about where machine learning belongs in a regulated workflow. A language model is well suited to interpretation and to language. It is poorly suited to being the component that decides whether a child's record may be disclosed, because its behaviour cannot be enumerated, tested exhaustively, or explained to a privacy officer in terms they can audit.

So the boundary is drawn explicitly. Deterministic code holds every decision with a consequence. Any model in the system operates strictly downstream of those decisions, on numbers it did not compute, and it cannot alter what was released.

Provenance as a first-class field

Every extracted fact records where it came from, what confidence the extractor assigned, and the exact phrase it was taken from. A measurement parsed from prose is labelled as such and never presented in the same visual register as a value read from a structured field.

In clinical research the difference between a documented finding and a software inference is the difference between evidence and a hypothesis. A system that blurs them produces results nobody should act on. Because the source phrase travels with the number, a clinician can adjudicate any single value in about two seconds, which is what makes the extracted axis usable rather than merely impressive.

Small cohorts are the hard case, and they are the point

In pediatric and rare disease research, scientific value and re-identification risk rise together. The cohorts most worth finding are the ones most easily identified. A node reporting exactly one patient with a rare presentation may have disclosed that patient.

Lantern suppresses results below a configurable k-anonymity threshold and returns a count band with a petition pathway instead of records. The threshold is returned in every API response rather than hidden inside the system, so any consumer can see the rule being applied to them.

Suppression alone is insufficient. An attacker can approach a protected count by asking two nearly identical questions and subtracting. Our defence fingerprints queries and detects that pattern, and it does so per hospital rather than across the network, because a safe federation-wide difference can conceal an unsafe single-institution one. In our own data a pair differing by one constraint isolated nine studies at one hospital while the network-wide difference was seventeen, comfortably above the threshold. Assessed federation-wide the attack succeeds. Assessed per node it is caught.

Disclosure risk belongs to a hospital cohort, not to the federation's sum. Getting that wrong is an easy mistake to make and an expensive one to discover later.

Statistical honesty about what we measured

We built the naive baseline and ran it rather than asserting an advantage. Literal keyword search over the same corpus recovers between 60 and 72 percent of the studies our compiled index finds for a reduced ejection fraction query, at precision between 9 and 24 percent, because words like reduced and severe attach to many findings.

For threshold queries we report the baseline as not expressible rather than as recall of zero. That distinction is deliberate and it is the heart of the argument. A keyword search does not perform poorly at ordering a measurement. It cannot express the question.

Our evaluation also records a case where plain keyword matching is more precise than our curated ontology expansion. We left that finding in, because a comparison that only flatters its author is not a measurement.

Where we were wrong

Our original demonstration query filtered for pediatric brain studies with an atrial width above 10 mm. Run against the real data it returned nothing at all, for two reasons we had not checked. Atrial width is a fetal measurement rather than a general brain one, and two of the three nodes hold no pediatric patients.

The corrected query is stronger than the original. Fetal lateral ventricular atrial width above 10 mm is the diagnostic threshold for fetal ventriculomegaly, so it is a question a fetal medicine specialist recognises immediately, and it is well represented across all three institutions. The severity stratification that follows from it (mild, moderate, severe) is clinically standard and produces genuinely small cohorts, which means our privacy demonstration runs on real clinical structure rather than a contrived filter.

A related error is worth recording because it is the kind a domain expert catches instantly. Fetal studies in this corpus carry the mother's age in the patient age field. Banding that chronologically labels a fetus an adult. Passports now derive gestational age from the report and state which basis was used, and the system says unknown rather than guessing.

What we did not build

No image analysis, because there are no images. No acquisition-parameter indexing, because those fields are absent. No language model in the request path, so natural-language input is validated rather than interpreted. No live retrieval endpoint, so approval returns a simulated grant. Burned-in pixel text handling is designed and not shipped, because nothing in this corpus can exercise it.

Each passport carries a field listing what it cannot support. A researcher deciding whether to commit six months to a cohort is better served by an accurate inventory than an impressive one, and naming the gap is what separates a research instrument from a demonstration.
