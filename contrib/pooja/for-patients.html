For patients and families

If your child has had a scan, two questions probably matter to you. Is anyone studying this condition, and where are my child's images? These are different questions and they have different answers.

Is my child's condition being studied?

You can search this system in plain language and find out whether research imaging exists for a condition, and roughly how much. You will see condition names as people say them rather than as codes, along with a broad age range.

You will not see any individual record, including your own child's. Nothing here identifies a patient, and that is true for everyone using it, at every level of access.

Why the numbers are sometimes vague, on purpose

For rare conditions, a precise count can identify a person. If one hospital reports holding exactly one child with an unusual finding, that sentence may describe a specific family.

So below a certain size, the system deliberately stops being precise. It will say a small group exists rather than naming a number. This is not the system being unhelpful. It is the system protecting the same children it is trying to help.

The conditions where research matters most are often the ones where the fewest children are affected, which is exactly when a number can point at a person.

Where are my child's images? A separate path entirely

Getting a copy of your own child's imaging is not a research question, and this system does not treat it as one. It is a right of access, and it runs through the hospital that holds the record, with proper identity verification.

That path is separate by design. Research discovery is de-identified and open. Access to your own records is identified, authenticated, and yours. Mixing those two would weaken both.

In practice: contact the hospital that performed the scan and ask for your records. If you would like copies sent to a researcher or an application, in many places you can direct that too. Your hospital's health information or medical records department is the right first call.

What happens to a scan that is shared for research

Before anything about a study leaves the hospital, identifying details are removed. Name, medical record number, and date of birth are taken out. Exact ages become broad ranges. The written report stays at the hospital.

What travels is a structured summary: the type of scan, the part of the body, the condition in standard medical vocabulary, and specific measurements a radiologist recorded. Researchers can search those measurements. They cannot read the report, and they cannot see the images without a separate, reviewed request that goes back to the hospital and is permanently logged.

Why this is worth doing

Children with uncommon conditions are the hardest group to study, because no single hospital sees enough of them. Tools that could help measure whether a treatment is working need examples from many children across many hospitals.

The purpose of a system like this is to let that research happen without any child's private information moving anywhere it should not. Both halves of that sentence are the point.
